You signed on as a contractor. You get a 1099 instead of a W-2, nothing is withheld, and there are no benefits. But you work the schedule they set, with their equipment, under their supervision, alongside people doing the same job who are on payroll. What your paperwork says and what your job looks like are not always the same.

 

A Contractor Is Supposed to Be Independent

That is the whole idea behind the arrangement. You decide how the work gets done, you can take other clients, you bring your own tools, you set your own hours. If someone else decides all of that for you, the relationship may not match the label on the paperwork, whatever the contract you signed happens to call it.

 

It Is Not Just a Tax Question

Most people first notice it in April, when the whole tax bill lands on them instead of being split. But the classification reaches further than that. Overtime, unemployment benefits, workers’ compensation if you get hurt on the job—a lot of what employees have access to is tied to being classified as one.

 

Write Down How the Work Really Runs

Who sets your hours. Whose equipment you use. Whether you can turn down a job or work for anyone else. Who trains you, who you report to, how long this has been going on. Keep your contract and your 1099s. That picture of the day-to-day is what anyone would actually look at, and it is easier to describe now than after the work ends.
