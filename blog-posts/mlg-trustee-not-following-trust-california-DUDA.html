By Steven McLellan, Esq. and Claire Melehani, Esq.

Bottom Line: A California trustee must administer the trust according to its terms, including creating and funding any sub-trusts the document requires after a settlor dies. When a trustee does not, beneficiaries can demand the trust terms, information, and an accounting, and then ask the probate court to compel the trustee to act, redress losses, or step aside.

The Trustee's First Duty: Follow the Document

Every other duty a trustee owes starts with this one. The Probate Code puts it plainly:

"On acceptance of the trust, the trustee has a duty to administer the trust according to the trust instrument and, except to the extent the trust instrument provides otherwise, according to this division."

Probate Code section 16000 (full text)

A trust usually asks different things of the trustee at different times. Some provisions apply while the settlor is alive, others take effect at death, and others govern sub-trusts that may last for decades for children or grandchildren. The period right after a settlor dies is when a trustee is expected to carry out the actions the document requires, and it is also when deviations most often begin.

The Duties That Travel With It

Section 16000 sits alongside a set of related duties. Each one is a separate obligation, and a violation of any duty the trustee owes a beneficiary is a breach of trust (Prob. Code, sec. 16400).

Probate Code sectionWhat it requires
16002Administer the trust solely in the interest of the beneficiaries (duty of loyalty).
16003Deal impartially with two or more beneficiaries.
16004Do not use trust property for the trustee's own profit or take part in transactions adverse to the beneficiaries (conflicts and self-dealing).
16006Take and keep control of, and preserve, the trust property.
16009Keep trust property separate and see that it is designated as property of the trust.
16060 and 16062Keep beneficiaries reasonably informed and, with exceptions, account at least annually.

Mandatory Sub-Trusts: Where Many Problems Start

Many estate plans for married couples divide the trust at the first death into separate sub-trusts, such as a survivor's trust, a bypass (or credit shelter) trust, or a marital trust. Each sub-trust can have different beneficiaries, different distribution rules, and different tax treatment, so whether the division actually happens matters a great deal to the people who inherit later.

"Shall" versus "may"

The wording of the trust usually tells you whether the division is required. As a general drafting convention, a provision saying the trustee shall divide or create a sub-trust is mandatory, while one saying the trustee may do so leaves it to the trustee's discretion. Every document has to be read as a whole, so this is a starting point, not a final answer.

Creating a sub-trust is not just paperwork

A sub-trust exists in practice only when it is funded: accounts opened in the sub-trust's name, assets moved into them, and real property deeded to it. That is consistent with the trustee's duty to keep trust property separate and designated as trust property (Prob. Code, sec. 16009). A surviving spouse who keeps everything in the original accounts for years, as if nothing changed, may be administering the trust contrary to its terms.

Common Ways Trustees Deviate From the Trust

  • Never creating or funding required sub-trusts after the first settlor dies.
  • Treating trust property as their own, including personal loans from the trust or using trust real estate without paying for it.
  • Making distributions the document does not allow, or withholding distributions it requires.
  • Favoring one beneficiary over others without authority in the document.
  • Going silent: no trust copy, no information, and no accountings.

How Beneficiaries Can Find Out What Is Happening

The only reliable way to know whether a trust is being followed is to see the documents. Beneficiaries can generally request:

  1. The terms of the trust. On request, the trustee must provide them, subject to limited exceptions (Prob. Code, sec. 16060.7).
  2. Information about the administration. On reasonable request, the trustee must report information relevant to the beneficiary's interest (Prob. Code, sec. 16061).
  3. An accounting. With exceptions, the trustee must account at least annually, at termination, and on a change of trustee to beneficiaries entitled to current distributions (Prob. Code, sec. 16062).

Put requests in writing. If a trustee fails to provide requested information or a requested account within 60 days after a written request, and none was provided in the prior six months, the beneficiary can petition the court to compel it (Prob. Code, sec. 17200, subd. (b)(7)). Every accounting also starts a three-year period for claims based on what it discloses, so read each one when it arrives.

What the Court Can Do

A beneficiary can petition the probate court on the internal affairs of the trust, including to instruct the trustee, compel the trustee to act, and compel redress of a breach (Prob. Code, sec. 17200). When a trustee commits or threatens a breach of trust, the remedies include:

  • Compelling the trustee to perform the trustee's duties, such as creating and funding required sub-trusts.
  • Enjoining a threatened breach.
  • Requiring the trustee to redress the breach by payment of money or otherwise.
  • Appointing a receiver or temporary trustee.
  • Setting aside the trustee's acts, or reducing or denying the trustee's compensation.
  • Removing the trustee.

These remedies are listed in Probate Code section 16420. A beneficiary's remedies against a trustee are equitable (Prob. Code, sec. 16421), and a trustee who breaches the trust can be charged with resulting losses, profits the trustee made, and profits the trust lost (Prob. Code, sec. 16440). Breach of trust is also a ground for removal (Prob. Code, sec. 15642).

Not every deviation calls for litigation. A trustee who simply did not understand the document will sometimes correct course after a clear letter. Others need a court order. Frequently Asked Questions

Does a California trustee have to follow the trust document?

Yes. Under Probate Code section 16000, on acceptance of the trust the trustee has a duty to administer the trust according to the trust instrument and, except to the extent the instrument provides otherwise, according to the Probate Code.

What happens if a trustee does not create the sub-trusts the trust requires?

Failing to create and fund sub-trusts that the trust document requires is a failure to administer the trust according to its terms. A beneficiary can ask the probate court to compel the trustee to act, to redress any resulting loss, or in appropriate cases to remove the trustee.

How can a beneficiary find out whether the trust is being followed?

Ask for it in writing. A trustee must provide the terms of the trust on request, report requested information relevant to the beneficiary's interest, and, for beneficiaries entitled to current distributions, account at least annually. If the trustee does not respond within 60 days of a written request, the beneficiary can petition the court.

What can the court do when a trustee breaches the trust?

Under Probate Code section 16420 a court can compel the trustee to perform, enjoin a breach, require the trustee to redress the breach by payment of money or otherwise, appoint a temporary trustee, set aside the trustee's acts, reduce or deny compensation, or remove the trustee.
