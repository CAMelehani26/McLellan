What Is a Trust Accounting?

Understanding Trust Accountings in California

A trustee manages someone else's money, and California law requires the trustee to show the work.

A trustee has a duty to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed about the trust and its administration (Probate Code § 16060). On top of that, Probate Code § 16062 requires a formal accounting at least annually, when the trust terminates, and whenever there is a change of trustee, to each beneficiary who is entitled to current distributions or may receive them in the trustee's discretion.

The Probate Code spells out what an accounting must contain.

Under Probate Code § 16063, an accounting has to include:

  • Receipts and disbursements of principal and income for the period.
  • The trust's assets and liabilities at the end of the period.
  • What the trustee paid themselves in compensation.
  • The agents the trustee hired, any relationship to the trustee, and what they were paid.
  • A statement that the beneficiary may ask the court to review the account and the trustee's acts.
  • A statement that claims for breach of trust are barred three years after the beneficiary receives an account disclosing the facts.

A spreadsheet or a bank statement forwarded by email is not the same thing. Missing pieces are often the first sign that something deserves a closer look.

Every accounting starts a clock, so read it when it arrives.

The three-year warning in every accounting is real. Some trusts also set a shorter period to object in writing, which can be as short as 180 days when the trustee gives the notice required by Probate Code § 16461. Putting an accounting in a drawer can cost a beneficiary the right to challenge what it discloses.

When a trustee will not account, the court can order it.

If a trustee does not provide a requested account within 60 days after a beneficiary's written request, and no account was provided in the six months before the request, the beneficiary can petition the probate court to compel one under Probate Code § 17200. Even when the trust document waives accountings, the court may still compel one if it is reasonably likely that a material breach has occurred (Probate Code § 16064).

An accounting either answers the question or becomes the evidence.

A complete, accurate accounting often resolves a beneficiary's concerns. One that is late, incomplete, or does not add up can support a petition to surcharge the trustee for losses, reduce the trustee's compensation, or remove the trustee (Probate Code § 16420; Probate Code § 15642). We look at what the numbers show before recommending which of those steps, if any, makes sense.

A trust accounting is the trustee's written report on how the trust has been managed. Under Probate Code section 16063 it must show receipts and disbursements of principal and income, the trust's assets and liabilities, the trustee's compensation, and the agents the trustee hired and what they were paid. It must also tell the beneficiary that they can ask the court to review the account and the trustee's acts.

Probate Code section 16062 requires the trustee to account at least annually, when the trust terminates, and when there is a change of trustee.
