What Is a Will Contest?

Understanding Will Contests in California

A will contest is a formal legal challenge to the validity of a deceased person's will.

It's typically filed by an heir, beneficiary, or other interested party who believes the document does not reflect the decedent's true intentions. Rather than disputing how assets are divided, a contest attacks the document itself, arguing it should not be admitted to probate, or should be set aside, because of the circumstances under which it was signed.

California law recognizes several grounds for contesting a will.

Common grounds include:

  • Lack of testamentary capacity.
  • Undue influence by a caregiver or family member.
  • Fraud.
  • Duress.
  • Improper execution under the formalities required by the Probate Code.

Suspicious circumstances, such as a late-in-life change favoring one child over others, or a will drafted shortly after a new relationship began, often prompt closer scrutiny.

How Long Do You Have to Contest a Will in California?

California imposes strict deadlines, and a contest filed after a will has already been admitted to probate faces a significantly shorter window than one filed before admission. Waiting too long can permanently forfeit your right to challenge a document you believe is invalid, which is why early legal guidance is critical.

Will Contest FAQs

California allows a will to be contested on grounds including lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence, fraud, duress, mistake, and improper execution. A successful contest typically requires more than suspicion, courts look for concrete evidence, such as medical records showing cognitive decline or communications revealing manipulation by a caregiver or family member.

There are two separate routes with different timing, and which one applies depends on where the case is. Before the will is admitted to probate, an interested person can contest it by filing an objection in the probate proceeding (Probate Code §8004). There is no fixed number of days attached to this route. What matters is that it happens before the court admits the will.

After the will has been admitted, the deadline is firm: 120 days from the date of admission to petition to revoke the probate. There is one exception. A person who was a minor, or who was incompetent and had no guardian or conservator when the will was admitted, may petition at any time before the order for final distribution is entered.

One limitation worth knowing: the 120-day route is not open to someone who was already a party to a will contest, or who had actual notice of one in time to join it and did not. You cannot stay out of the first contest and then use this deadline as a second opportunity.

Undue influence occurs when someone uses their position of trust, often a caregiver, new spouse, or close family member, to overcome the person's free will and produce an estate-plan result shaped by the influencer rather than the decedent's independent decision-making. Courts examine the decedent's dependency on the influencer, the fairness of the outcome, and whether the influencer was involved in procuring the will.

Only an 'interested party' may contest a will. Standing generally requires an interest that would be affected by whether the challenged will is admitted or invalidated, such as an heir or beneficiary who would receive a different inheritance under a prior will or intestacy. A person with no financial stake in the outcome typically cannot bring a contest.

If a court invalidates a will, the estate is typically distributed under a prior valid will, or if none exists, according to California's intestate succession laws. In some cases, only the specific provision found invalid, such as a suspicious late amendment, is struck while the rest of the document stands.

California enforces no-contest clauses only in limited circumstances, and courts construe them narrowly. For a direct contest, California generally will not enforce a no-contest clause when the contestant had probable cause, but different rules may apply to certain property-ownership challenges and creditor claims expressly covered by the clause. An attorney should review the specific clause and the strength of your claim before you file.
